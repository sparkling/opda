# Claims, evidence and the provenance of property information

For property professionals, data stewards and implementers.

A property record can contain a seller's answer, a local authority result, an extracted document value and a professional assessment. Putting them in one pack does not give them a common origin or the same evidential force. A useful account preserves what is asserted, what supports it, who produced it and how it reached the recipient. Provenance makes those relationships inspectable; it does not remove the need to assess the evidence.

The retained OPDA material approaches this at two stages of the programme. Historical ODR0009 analysed claims and verification in the schema-derived PDTF ontology. The later ODR0052 adopts a method for provenance, attribution and extraction quality in new modelling. They share useful concepts but have different authority: the historical model is input evidence, while the accepted method explicitly records implementation as pending. Neither establishes that every part of the current Property Pack candidate already implements these patterns.

## Four different things in one apparently simple answer

Imagine a seller says that an extension received approval. A scanned document contains an application reference and a decision. A software tool extracts those details. A conveyancer considers the document alongside other evidence. The claim, document, extraction and professional assessment are related, but they are not interchangeable.

The seller's statement records what that person asserts. The document provides evidence with its own issuer, date and scope. Extraction creates a new representation of some document content. The professional assessment answers a question within a particular responsibility. A confidence value produced by the extractor concerns that extraction; it does not establish the legal sufficiency of the approval or the correctness of the seller's wider account.

This is an illustrative teaching example. Its value lies in showing where responsibility and meaning change as information moves. A single label such as “verified” would conceal which check was performed, by whom and against what evidence.

## Entities, activities and agents

ODR0052 selects a bounded PROV-O vocabulary. An entity is information whose provenance is being described, an activity is something that produced or used information, and an agent is a responsible person, organisation or software agent. A source document and an extracted record can therefore be distinct entities connected through an extraction activity associated with a tool.

The activity may identify a plan or procedure and the software agent may record a version. These details allow a later reviewer to understand how a value was obtained. They also help scope a correction: if a particular extractor version misread a type of date, the affected derived records can be investigated without declaring every fact from the original document unreliable.

The method keeps a source-file path distinct from a location resource. This is a small but instructive semantic boundary. A literal path is text describing where a file was recorded; a relationship to a location connects resources. Similar-looking annotations cannot safely be combined merely because they are both useful to a user interface.

## Attribution at the right level

Sometimes all statements in an immutable record share the same origin and extraction confidence. In that bounded case, resource-level attribution can be economical and truthful. The method permits it when the resource is created and validated as an immutable unit.

Now suppose a person corrects one address component using a different source. The record no longer has a uniform origin. Retaining one resource-wide extraction confidence would imply more than the evidence supports. ODR0052 therefore calls for selective statement qualification when origins or confidence differ, using its chosen RDF1.2 reification approach. This is a method commitment, not a claim that a deployed editor already performs the transition automatically.

The practical trade-off is between unnecessary detail and lost accountability. Reifying every statement increases complexity even where all statements share one origin. Attributing only the whole record becomes misleading when information has been combined or edited. The point of the hybrid approach is to match provenance granularity to the actual evidence.

## Evidence and assurance are related but distinct

Historical ODR0009 separates PROV-O lineage from information about verification. It distinguishes documents, electronic records and personal vouches, while later amendments recast evidence as a role a bearer plays in verification. The earlier evidence-subclass tree was retired in favour of a coded classification and value-dependent obligations. This history shows why the nature of a document and its use as evidence require different descriptions.

The same record's July 2026 amendment removed an assurance-level term after an audit found no supporting source field. An outcome such as pass, fail or consider was not evidence of an assurance grade such as Low, Substantial or High. Repairing a property's structure had not supplied the missing meaning. This is a concrete example of removing an attractive modelling claim when the source does not justify it; the earlier assurance-level proposal must not be taught as an active capability.

Those distinctions remain valuable teaching material even where a new model chooses a different implementation. Knowing that a document was used is different from knowing which check it passed. A digest can help establish whether bytes have changed; it does not establish whether the issuer's statement was correct. A recognisable source can be authoritative for some subjects without being competent to answer every downstream question.

An identity check followed by another check is also a chain of activities, not a bag of unrelated “verified” flags. Historical ODR0009 illustrates dependencies between identity, anti-money-laundering and source-of-funds verification. That account explains why evidence about an earlier activity may matter to a later one. It is not a statement of current legal requirements or permission to omit professional checks.

## Keeping four histories separate

ODR0052 identifies four provenance questions. Extraction origin explains how content was obtained. Editorial history explains how a modeller interpreted or changed a definition. Mapping-decision provenance explains why two meanings were linked. Discovery-system origin explains where a search or catalogue found something. A record can need all four.

For example, finding a planning document through a data platform does not make the platform its original author. An analyst mapping the document's terminology to a local concept is making a further interpretive decision. If that mapping changes, the original document need not change. Keeping the histories separate makes it possible to correct the interpretation while preserving the evidence.

## What a recipient gains

The DPMSG trust-framework explainer proposes carrying a property's source, timestamp, version, provenance and trust indicators with the data. Its approved-extension and energy-certificate examples show why the receiver needs more than the value. A recipient can decide whether the source is relevant, whether the record is sufficiently current and what additional work is required for its purpose.

This also makes disagreement manageable. Two claims can be retained with their different origins while a responsible participant investigates the discrepancy. A later correction can be linked to the earlier record instead of silently overwriting the history. These are useful design consequences of explicit provenance, not evidence that the current candidate already supplies a complete dispute-resolution service.

The strongest trust claim is therefore specific. It identifies the statement, supporting evidence, activity and responsible participant, and states the relevant limits. That precision helps professionals reuse information while retaining their judgement and makes software assistance easier to audit when it makes a mistake.
