# OPDA's purpose and the people who need shared property data

For property professionals and programme participants.

Buying, selling and financing a property involves several professions looking at the same property for different reasons. An estate agent needs information that helps market it accurately. A conveyancer investigates legal matters. A surveyor describes condition or value for a particular purpose. A lender assesses a lending proposition. A search or data provider supplies evidence, while technology providers carry information between systems. The practical problem is not simply that each party has a different computer. The same words, identifiers and documents can carry different meanings, responsibilities and limits.

The Open Property Data Association brings organisations across this chain together around better property information and interoperability. Its recorded founding cohort dates from 2023. That history explains part of the programme's origin; it is not a current membership register. The retained 2026 Constitution describes Founder, Certified and Associate membership, while the website's membership account emphasises equal benefits and a single tier. These source-specific descriptions must not be converted into an invented voting rule. Founding history, later membership and technical certification are different descriptions, and none establishes automatic authority over a new model decision.

## A useful shared account

Consider an illustrative transaction in which an energy certificate is supplied early. An agent may need its rating for a listing, a lender may consider energy performance, and another participant may need the certificate's address or property identifier. Copying a rating into several systems does not by itself establish that all parties are referring to the same property, certificate or observation. A useful shared account retains what the value means, what it concerns, where it came from and the conditions under which it can be used.

Shared meaning does not erase professional judgement. A search result is not a conveyancer's legal advice. A survey observation is not automatically a lending decision. A source document can support several uses without transferring its author's responsibility to every consumer. This is why the Development work asks for explicit resources, relationships, source evidence and domain ownership, rather than a universal list of fields with assumed meanings.

## Organisation, technical work and scheme development

The existing PDTF schema is a Digital Property Pack JSON Schema package and supporting material. It offers practical evidence about exchange structures and existing implementation needs. The separately derived ontology makes aspects of that material inspectable as concepts and relationships. SPDTF, the Smart Property Data Trust Framework, is the first scheme draft being authored collaboratively across industry and stakeholders. In the maintained programme account it remains in development, not adopted.

OPDA convening, a member's technical capability and the authority of an SPDTF candidate are therefore separate matters. A firm's experience can expose a missing requirement or an impractical rule. It does not make that firm's preferred interpretation the industry's agreed meaning. Equally, a working-group discussion is valuable evidence but does not become a release merely because participants found it persuasive.

The website also records a Trust Framework Sandbox delivered through OPDA, the Council for Licensed Conveyancers and Raidiam. That delivery account supplies evidence about operating arrangements. It must retain its own scope and date. Sandbox participation is not proof that the draft Property Pack ontology has been adopted, that a participant conforms to every future SPDTF requirement, or that government has approved a statutory property scheme.

## How different participants contribute

Domain specialists contribute definitions, examples and difficult cases. Implementers show how data is actually represented and where systems lose information. Governance participants make responsibilities, review states and decisions explicit. Consumers identify which distinctions matter to a real decision. The value of collaboration comes from connecting these contributions while preserving who knows what, what the evidence supports and what still needs agreement.

## What the association's own sources add

The retained Constitution describes an open property-data ecosystem underpinned by trust infrastructure. It gives the association a role in collaboration, open standards, education and innovation, and describes responsibilities for the Executive Committee, Board and General Assembly. Specific activities can be delegated to working groups. The practical consequence is that an active project needs a recorded mandate, not merely an enthusiastic chair or a working Microsoft Team. A participant's ability to contribute and a body's authority to ratify are different questions.

The Constitution also limits how membership is represented: a member may publicise membership but must not imply endorsement of its products or services. That distinction matters when the programme describes examples supplied by vendors. A helpful implementation demonstrates something about a specific exchange or product. It does not establish that every product claim has been checked by OPDA, or that a new candidate model has acquired certification through association with that firm.

The 2026 introductory presentation adds a consumer perspective. It reports the 2025 survey as showing interest in digital property packs alongside concerns about security, control and trusted participants. Those are reported survey findings, not proof that a particular technology will remove transaction delays. They explain why a successful information service needs more than speed: people also need to understand who receives their data, why it is requested and what evidence supports it.

## An ecosystem rather than one central application

The same presentation describes schemas and APIs as complementary tools. A schema describes a payload's structure. An API offers operations through which software can submit or retrieve information. Neither alone decides whether an organisation is allowed to obtain a record or whether a value remains suitable for its proposed use. Those questions bring provenance, policy and operating controls into the design.

For example, an illustrative conveyancing platform may be able to retrieve an energy rating but still need the certificate's identity, date and property association before presenting it as relevant evidence. A lender may then apply its own assessment rules. Keeping those steps visible avoids both duplicated collection and misplaced confidence. The programme's potential lies in making the hand-offs intelligible across independent organisations; it does not require every organisation to operate the same application or surrender its professional responsibilities.
