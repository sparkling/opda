# Consumer experience and the economic case for better property information

For consumer advocates, property professionals and programme decision makers.

A person buying a home experiences the transaction as one major decision. The information supporting it passes through many organisations, each with a narrower responsibility. An estate agent, conveyancer, lender and search provider may all be working diligently while the buyer is repeatedly asked for documents and cannot discover what is holding up the move. Better property information matters when it changes that experience: an issue becomes visible earlier, a request has a clear purpose, or evidence can be reused without losing its meaning.

The retained research offers three complementary perspectives. OPDA's consumer report describes experiences and expectations. Santander's *Fixing the Broken Chain* examines failed transactions and their wider effects. The March 2026 research commissioned by the Department for Business and Trade models the potential costs and benefits of Smart Data use cases. A survey response, an estimate of existing harm and a forecast of future benefits answer different questions. Together they support an informed case for change without demonstrating that a particular ontology has already produced those benefits.

## What consumers say is difficult

OPDA's *The Future of Homebuying* reports that 62.3% of respondents were asked to provide the same information or documents two or three times. It reports 40.6% identifying communication between stakeholders as the most challenging aspect and 39.5% describing the burden of chasing updates. These findings give substance to “friction”: people spend time reconstructing information, discovering who has it and trying to understand whether something has changed.

The methodology matters. Censuswide surveyed 5,000 people during October 2024 who had bought or sold in the preceding five years. OPDA and its members also distributed the questionnaire to 336 contacts through SurveyMonkey. The executive summary reports the combined 5,336 respondents. Those two collection routes should remain visible; the total should not be described as one uniformly sampled population.

The same report records positive responses to the idea of digital property packs: 82.2% viewed the concept positively and 76.7% would be willing to use such technology for secure information sharing. These are reported attitudes towards a proposed service. They do not establish actual uptake, willingness to pay a specific price, or the effect of a particular implementation. A useful design question follows: what must a service do to make the promised security, control and usefulness understandable to someone moving home?

## From repeated documents to usable evidence

Consider an illustrative buyer who receives a seller's statement about flooding, an environmental search and an insurance question. These may all concern flood risk but do not say the same thing. The seller may report personal knowledge of past events; a search may assess a location using a particular dataset; an insurer may ask a question for its own underwriting purpose. A digital service that collapses these into one unqualified “flood risk” value could reduce typing while making the decision less reliable.

The Property Pack evidence baseline contains just such distinctions. The source workbook separately asks about flood defences, knowledge of flood risk and whether the property has flooded in the last five years. Retaining their subjects, evidence and time boundaries makes reuse more valuable. A participant can see which question has already been answered, what that answer supports and whether another enquiry is still justified. Reuse need not mean that every professional accepts an answer without assessment.

## The cost of a failed transaction

Santander commissioned WPI Economics for economic analysis and J.L. Partners for consumer research. The report describes an online survey of 2,363 respondents in July 2025, oversampling people who had experienced failed transactions and applying quotas and weighting. Its methodology states a 2% margin of error. Its estimates of economic cost use a theory of change connecting failed transactions to direct expenditure, lost work, leisure time and wellbeing.

The report estimates around 530,000 failed transactions annually in England and Wales and approximately £1.5 billion in associated losses to consumers and the economy. These are research estimates, not a count of losses recorded by the Property Pack programme. Nor is that whole amount automatically recoverable through improved data. Changed circumstances, financing, preferences and chains can cause failures even when information is excellent.

Its broader contribution is to show why the outcome cannot be reduced to administrative minutes saved. Uncertainty affects wellbeing, willingness to move again and whether households can reach housing that suits their circumstances. These consequences justify asking consumers about clarity, confidence and repeated effort alongside transaction duration. They also caution against treating the fastest possible completion as the only measure of success: discovering a serious problem early may appropriately stop a purchase.

## Who pays, who benefits and when

The PSC's March 2026 Smart Data research models digital information for homebuying over 2028–2043. It estimates £14.1 billion net present value for that use case under its assumptions. The report separately gives a broader homebuying scheme extrapolation and an annual GDP estimate. Those figures are different measures with different boundaries; they must not be added together or presented as current cash savings.

Its proposed mechanism is secure sharing of verified property information through authorised third parties. Potential benefits include less administrative work, fewer failed transactions and improved financial-services liquidity. Major costs include digitising government-held property records, creating APIs and maintaining information over time. A local authority or data holder may incur substantial investment while benefits spread across consumers, intermediaries and the wider economy. That distribution creates a coordination and funding problem that a data model alone cannot solve.

The technical annex makes uncertainty concrete. An assumed 70% reduction in time spent on paperwork and administration was triangulated from four interviewees. The model combines fixed estimates with probability distributions for uncertain inputs. It is therefore useful for examining scenarios and dependencies, not for promising an individual buyer a 70% reduction. The report also excludes some data flows from its homebuying calculation, so its scope is narrower than every possible use of a comprehensive property record.

## Turning the case into an adoption question

A credible pilot would connect an intervention to an observable result. For example: did earlier access to a qualified search result reduce repeated requests; did people understand who supplied it; and did it expose a problem before money was committed? Measuring only the number of records exchanged would miss those outcomes. Measuring only completion time could hide adverse selection or differences in transaction complexity.

Implementation also needs to work for people who use professional assistance rather than a digital portal themselves. The retained MHCLG roadmap explicitly discusses preserving non-digital options while professionals benefit from consistent information. That policy intention belongs beside the enthusiasm for digital tools: participation in a better information system need not require every consumer to become an expert operator.

The economic case is strongest when benefits, costs, assumptions and responsibilities remain connected. Consumer evidence identifies worthwhile problems. A model explains a possible mechanism and its dependencies. A bounded implementation can then establish what changed in practice. The current Property Pack candidate remains a machine-proposed semantic resource; its contribution is to make information and responsibilities clearer enough for that practical work to be undertaken and assessed.
